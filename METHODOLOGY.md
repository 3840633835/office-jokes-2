# Methodology — One-Line Office Jokes (#56)

Competition: `u-markk-st-one-line-office-jokes-a51f2bcc`, $2, 5 paid spots
(1st $0.73, 2nd $0.50, 3rd $0.35, 4th $0.25, 5th $0.17). Gate: 1–199
characters, one line, not only white space. Blind rounds; final at the
deadline 2026-11-27 13:25 CST.

## The judge's brief (from RULES.txt, in priority order)

1. A single-line joke about office life, not a story, slogan or list.
2. Turns on a real detail of office life (meetings, email, printer,
   coffee machine) rather than generic talk about work.
3. Lands its laugh in the last few words, so the turn arrives on the
   final beat.
4. Clear on one reading, with no setup that needs explaining.
5. Fresh wording rather than a joke already going round the internet.

Ties on Judge win rate go to the smaller text (fewer bytes), then to the
earlier receipt — so short is a real advantage.

## What to avoid

The earlier entry #43 (a different competition, "Make the AI Judge Laugh:
Office Jokes") used "Our lift stops at every floor but the top. Same as
my career." — the lift angle and that line are NOT reused here. Its
builder's 1,000-title sample showed the crowded angles: meetings/emails
(176), printer (37), performance review (32). This build avoids the lift
and steers away from the most crowded meeting/email wordings where a
fresher angle exists.

## Why #34 wins

#34, #35/#37 and #33 all score 46, but #34 wins the head-to-head:

- **Shortest of the top cluster: 48 characters / 48 bytes.** The rules
  send an exact tie to the smaller text, so 48 beats 64 (#37), 68 (#33)
  and 73 (#35) before the earlier-receipt rule ever matters.
- **Cleanest structure.** Two short sentences in parallel; the second
  negates the first. No setup, no clause to parse, one reading.
- **Real detail.** "Lumbar support" is a specific office-chair detail,
  not generic work talk; the second sentence anchors it to a career.
- **Punch on the final beat.** "does not" is the last two words.
- **Fresh.** The chair/career parallel is not a circulating office joke.
  #35/#37 leans on the crowded meeting angle (the #1 crowded angle in
  the #43 sample); #11/#12 are known email-phrase glosses; #30 is a
  known sticky-note trope with an escalation.
- **Not a repeat of #43.** Different line, different angle (chair, not
  lift), and no word shared with the #43 entry except the office frame.

## Chosen entry (48 chars / 48 bytes, no trailing newline)

> My chair has lumbar support. My career does not.
